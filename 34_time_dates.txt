PODCAST 34 — DATES, TIME ZONES, AND JAVASCRIPT
Approximate runtime: 5 minutes

Date and time bugs are among the most frustrating software bugs because they often work perfectly until a particular time zone, daylight-saving transition, or boundary date appears.

First decide what concept you are representing.

An instant in time is different from a calendar date. 'The meeting starts at 3 PM in New York' is different from 'the user's birthday is March 4.'

Store and transmit instants in a consistent representation, commonly using UTC-based timestamps.

Keep the original time zone when the business meaning requires it.

Be careful when parsing date strings. Ambiguous formats can produce different interpretations across environments.

Prefer standardized formats such as ISO 8601 when exchanging timestamps.

Do not assume every day contains the same number of hours. Daylight-saving transitions can create unusual local-time behavior.

Testing should include boundary dates and multiple time zones when time is important to the application.

JavaScript's Date API has historically had confusing behavior, so teams should establish conventions and use modern date/time tooling where appropriate.

Database timestamp types also matter. Understand whether a column represents an instant, a local timestamp, or a date.

Logging should make time zones clear when diagnosing production issues.

The central rule is to model time according to its meaning.

If the business concept is an instant, treat it as an instant. If it is a calendar date, do not accidentally turn it into a timestamp.

Time bugs usually begin as modeling bugs.
